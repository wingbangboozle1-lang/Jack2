Decision

The recommendation, the arithmetic behind it, eight triggers that would change it, and what would make holding right.

Decide on term, not on optimism

The asset is a bond with a building attached: its price is the rent divided by a cap rate, and the cap rate depends on how many years of contracted rent a buyer can see. The one thing the owner controls is how much term exists at the moment of sale. That is why the recommendation is to convert the 2030–2035 uncertainty into 2032 certainty — by extending, then selling — and why the fallback is to sell now rather than wait.

Why the leader is only barely ahead — and why that is the point

S3 leads S1 by 2.2%. That is inside the model's own error bars, so the honest conclusion is not "extend at any price". It is: the extension is worth having if it costs nothing. If the tenant demands a rent cut to extend, the arithmetic moves to sell-now. If the tenant will not engage, sell-now. Either way the owner exits before expiry, and the difference between the two exits is small compared with the difference between either of them and waiting.

Risk posture: what "manageable" means here

Downside is bounded by the sale, not by the lease

In the worst 5% of trials, holding to expiry ends at $1,170,392 versus $1,347,008 for extend-and-sell. The gap is the cost of carrying a single-tenant, single-asset, seismic-exposed building through an option that the tenant controls.

Upside is capped, and that is acceptable

The 95th percentile of S3 ($1,880,916) is barely above its median: net-lease income does not compound quickly. Buying a lottery ticket with the asset is not required to preserve wealth.

Liquidity is the real constraint

A single-tenant drive-through in a specific trade area has few buyers. A discretionary sale with term has a market; a forced sale of a vacant box has a land buyer.

The tax tool is free — use it

§1031 costs nothing but discipline and a qualified intermediary, and it is worth 4.4% of certainty-equivalent wealth here.

What would make holding the right answer

  • The tenant offers a long extension at or above in-place rent, with a corporate guaranty and CPI escalators — then holding through 2032 (S3) is the plan, not holding forever.
  • Cap rates compress to 5.25% or below while the 10-year falls: refinancing or a later sale could beat an immediate exit. The model tests this and still prefers selling with term.
  • The owner's basis is so low and the property so central to their estate plan that a §1031 exchange is not available — in which case a taxable sale is the only exit and the timing question becomes a tax-year question, not a market one.
  • Nothing in the verified data supports holding past 2035 with a single tenant whose equity is negative: S5 is last in every sensitivity cell tested.
