Decision

Sell — after trying to buy the term first

Do not let this lease run to expiry. Spend four weeks reading the lease and confirming the guarantor, six months trying to buy 10–15 more years of term from the franchisor, then go to market — with an extension if you got one, without it if you did not. Execute through a §1031 exchange.

Why: the passage of time alone costs about a quarter of the asset's value, the trade area is shrinking, the tenant is closing stores, and rates are rising against you. The expected cost of doing nothing is larger than the tax bill you are trying to avoid.

It is the correct answer only if the Owner can absorb the bottom decile without changing their standard of living. Four steps, in order

Step 0 — Resolve the unknowns (weeks 0–4, cost ≈ $0)

  1. The lease, redacted. Exact expiry, escalations, options (who holds them and at what rent), guarantor, landlord obligations, and any kick-out right.
  2. The estoppel certificate from the tenant — the same document a buyer will demand.
  3. The franchisee's identity and financial standing, and whether a Jack in the Box Inc. corporate guaranty exists.
  4. Current rent roll, CAM reconciliations, tax bill and insurance binder.

Expected value of this step: ~$500,000 (21.6% of the 2030-expiry value, on a $200,000-NOI asset). It is the highest-return action available by an enormous margin.

Step 1 — Test the extension (months 1–6)

Propose a 10–15 year extension and expect to pay for it. The model assumes a 4% rent concession and only a 55% probability the franchisor agrees at all; both are assumptions and both are the swing factors in strategy D.

  • If achievable at ≤ 8% rent concession, take it. That is still a large net gain against the 23.5% term premium.
  • If the franchisor wants > 15%, or a below-market option, walk. You would be transferring the premium to the tenant rather than creating it for the Owner.

Step 2 — Sell, in either case (months 6–18)

With an extension signed, market the asset with ~13–15 years of term (strategy D, the second-best risk-adjusted outcome at 11.06). Without one, market it as-is into the private-capital and §1031 buyer pool — the deepest bid for sub-$10m net-lease product.

Step 3 — Execute the §1031 exchange properly

  • Engage a qualified intermediary before closing. Under IRC §1031 the Owner cannot touch proceeds and cannot act as their own facilitator.
  • 45-day identification / 180-day completion. Not extendable except in a presidentially declared disaster. A missed deadline converts the entire deferred gain into a fully taxable one.
  • California caution (flagged): California does not defer California-source gain when replacement property sits outside California without an undertaking to file a California return when the replacement is later sold (FTB Form 3840). Not verified in this session — get an opinion before identifying replacement property if the plan is to leave California.
  • Spread proceeds across at least two assets to break the single-parcel concentration.

Step 4 — Hold only if every one of these is true

  • The lease is absolute net with a corporate guaranty
  • Escalations are ≥ 2%/yr — a flat lease during 3.72% inflation is a guaranteed real-terms loss
  • The extension is ≥ 15 years at market rent with no tenant-held renewal option
  • This asset is a small fraction of the Owner's net worth
  • The Owner can absorb a worst-decile outcome (≈ 1.88× NOI vs ≈ 11× if selling)

If even one box is unchecked, the hold case fails on the model's own numbers.

Each scenario replays 5,000 Monte-Carlo paths. The black swan is a joint shock: 10-year Treasury to 6.90%, zero rent growth, 25% renewal probability, 4.5% sale costs and land value at 6.5× NOI.

The ranking survives the bad state of the world. In the black-swan replay, holding loses 34.3% relative to selling and paying the tax. The recommendation does not flip — and it survives precisely because it removes exposure early.

Control test. With the catastrophe and default priors switched off, renew-and-hold rises to 11.33 from 9.62. Its apparent attractiveness comes entirely from the benign half of the distribution. That is the definition of tail risk, and it is why the certainty-equivalent ranking, not the mean, is the right basis for this decision.

This is not a rates call. Across a 350 bp range of terminal 10-year Treasury — 3.50% to 7.00% — selling via §1031 ranks first every time. The dominant terms are the term premium capture and the removal of single-asset tail risk, not the discount rate.

Where the answer does flip is the Owner's discount rate: at 6% the best strategy becomes D — extend, then sell, because an Owner with a low opportunity cost of capital should keep the asset long enough to capture the extension premium before selling. At 7% and above, A1 leads.

What would make this recommendation wrong

  1. The lease is absolute-net, corporate-guaranteed, with 3%/yr bumps and a market-rent renewal option. Then the asset is better than modelled — and that strengthens extend-then-sell, not hold-to-expiry.
  2. The true basis is much higher than assumed. A high basis lowers the tax on sale toward zero, which raises plain selling (A) above the §1031 route and makes selling outright even more attractive.
  3. There is a below-market strategic buyer — the franchisee, or an adjacent owner — willing to pay well above market. The model assumes an arm's-length sale.
  4. There is a genuine redevelopment play. A central-LA infill drive-thru parcel can be worth more as land for housing under current state law than as a 6.5%-cap QSR. This is the largest un-quantified upside in the dossier and could not be tested without an address. Long-term renewal would extinguish the option, so it must be tested before signing anything.
  5. An estate-planning reason to hold to death for a basis step-up. Not modelled, and appropriately so — it depends on facts this dossier should not hold.
