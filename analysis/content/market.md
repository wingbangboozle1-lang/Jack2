# Demographics, income & schools

## A dense, slowly shrinking, middle-income county

Los Angeles County had 9,694,934 residents on 1 July 2025 — down 3.2% from the 2020 census base. The City of Los Angeles had 3,869,089, down 0.8%. Both are shrinking, which is unusual for a major U.S. metro and matters for a retail asset: unit-level demand is a function of households, and the household count is not growing.

The density is still extraordinary: 8,304 people per square mile in the city, 3.4 times the county's 2,467. Dense residential fabric is what makes a drive-thru or walk-up QSR work, and it is why the site's land value has a floor even if the building becomes obsolete.

## Income is adequate, poverty is high, and both are stable

County median household income was $90,112 (2020–2024 ACS, 2024 dollars); the city median was $81,939, about 9% lower. Per-capita income was $45,792 county-wide and $47,685 in the city.

The poverty rate tells a different story: 13.3% county-wide and 16.5% in the city. One in six city residents lives below the poverty line. For a value-oriented burger brand this is not necessarily bad news — limited-service formats typically outperform in lower-income trade areas — but it does cap pricing power. A QSR that pushes menu prices faster than its customers' incomes will lose transactions, and the CPI data show exactly that happening: food away from home rose 2.8% over the year to September 2026 while headline CPI rose 3.7% and energy rose 20.3%.

## Consumer demand

The city recorded $81.35 billion of retail sales in 2022, or $21,281 per resident; the county recorded $22,010 per resident. Restaurants and accommodation accounted for $17.37 billion of city sales. That is the addressable market the site trades inside, and it is enormous — but it is also mature.

## Homelessness is a visible operating factor

LAHSA's January 2026 point-in-time count estimated 45,194 people experiencing homelessness in the City of Los Angeles, up 3.4% year on year, against 73,040 county-wide (+1.2%). Downtown/Skid Row rose sharply. For a central-LA restaurant this affects late-night trade, security cost, insurance and employee safety. The figure is recorded as **reported** rather than verified because the LAHSA report page itself was not retrievable in this pass; the number is LAHSA's, read through press coverage.

## Schools: an open item, deliberately not filled

School quality drives family retention and therefore long-run trade-area income. The California Department of Education's district profile endpoint returned an error during this pass, so no enrollment, graduation or proficiency figure is asserted. It would be easy to paste a third-party school rating here. That would be a non-official source presented as fact, which is exactly what this project is designed not to do. ## What this means for the decision

The trade area is dense, stable and middle-income, with high poverty and a shrinking population. It supports a QSR — but it does not support aggressive rent growth. A 2.5% contractual escalator against 3.7% CPI is a real-terms rent cut, and a tenant whose volumes are falling will eventually notice. That is a slow-bleed argument for selling into a market that still prices the lease, rather than holding until the market prices the box.
