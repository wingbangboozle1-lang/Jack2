Risk register

How to read this page

Risk is separated into two classes.

Business as usual (BAU) — the ordinary frictions of owning one net-leased building: tenant credit, rollover, interest rates, property tax resets, transfer tax, concentration. These are measurable, mostly priceable, and largely within the owner's power to avoid.

Black swans — low-probability, high-severity events that can physically damage the asset or reprice the entire local economy: earthquake, wildfire and smoke, insurance-market shock, civil disturbance, pandemic, utility failure, a California-specific regulatory shock.

Every row carries the evidence behind it and a link to the official source. Likelihood and impact are assessments by this project, clearly labelled — no official agency publishes a probability that a particular franchisee will default or that a particular parcel will burn.

The three risks that actually drive the decision

1. The tenant is probably not the brand (RK-01, RK-02). 93.0% of the Jack in the Box system is franchised. The owner's counterparty is most likely a single-store operator whose rent is a fixed cost against falling volume — system same-store sales were (4.2%) in FY2025 — while the franchisor closes 86 units in FY2025 and guides 50–100 more in FY2026. The model's 5%-a-year default hazard compounds to 18.8% by 2030 and 39.9% by 2035. This is the largest single driver of the difference between the strategies.

2. The owner does not hold the renewal (RK-04). Renewal options in QSR leases sit with the tenant. "Renew the lease" is therefore not a strategy the owner can choose; it is an outcome the owner can only influence by paying for it. Any analysis that frames the question as "sell or renew" has the agency backwards.

3. Cap rates have room to widen (RK-05). With the 10-year at 4.84%, the 30-year at 5.25% and the FOMC tightening again in September 2026, a 6.00% cap carries only a 116 bp spread. A +50 bp move costs 7.7% of value; +100 bp costs 14.3%. Waiting does not avoid this risk — it holds it.

Black swans: what is actually insurable

The Los Angeles basin sits in the highest class of the USGS National Seismic Hazard Model. A damaging earthquake is not a tail scenario in the way it would be in Ohio; it is a real, recurring hazard. Commercial earthquake cover is usually a separate policy with a percentage deductible, and code-upgrade and business-interruption gaps are common. The model applies a 1.5%-a-year probability and a 15% value loss — deliberately modest, because a severe event would exceed both.

Wildfire is the more visible hazard but the less direct one for an urban central-LA parcel. In 2025 CAL FIRE recorded 8,232 wildfires, 507,817 acres burned, 31 fatalities and 16,627 structures destroyed statewide, including the Palisades (23,448 acres) and Eaton (14,021 acres) fires in Los Angeles County, which triggered FEMA declaration DR-4856-CA on 8 January 2025. For a central-LA site the direct burn risk is low; the transmission channel is insurance. The FAIR Plan assessed its members $1 billion after the January 2025 fires, and commercial surplus-lines pricing moves with the same catastrophe load.

That is the honest structure of the black-swan problem here: the events that could destroy the local economy are mostly not the events that would destroy this building, but they are the events that would reprice its insurance, its tenants' cost base and its buyer pool.

What the register implies for the decision

A single-tenant asset concentrates every one of these risks into one lease, one building and one block. Diversification does not eliminate earthquake or wildfire exposure — a California portfolio still shakes and still burns — but it removes the idiosyncratic risk that this particular franchisee, on this particular corner, fails in this particular year. That is precisely the risk the model says is worth 130 bp of yield to shed.
